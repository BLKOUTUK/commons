Liberation
technology

How we do technology — and why the how is the point.

BLKOUT builds digital infrastructure for Black queer liberation. Not liberation as a theme. Liberation as a build standard. Against the extractive artificial intelligence of the platforms — which ranks, mines and erases — we ground our work in ancestral intelligence: the knowledge, survival craft and cultural forms carried by Black queer ancestors and elders, and this community's right to hold, interpret and transmit them itself.

Three words describe what that means in practice.

How it's developed

Ancestral

Grounded in what our ancestors and elders carried. Values- and rights-upholding from the first line of code, not audited in afterwards.

How it's delivered

Collaborative

Built with the community, governed cooperatively, owned through a Community Benefit Society with an asset lock. Never purely transactional.

What it leaves behind

Reparative

The outcome repairs erasure rather than extracts from it. Cultural memory returned to the people it belongs to.

The policy

What we will and
won't do with AI

Version one. Adopted by the board of BLKOUT Creative Ltd, 17 June 2026 — nem con.

Version two will be co-produced with members. The box at the foot of this page is where that starts.

The policy at a glance — seven principles

  1. 01

    People keep the promises. AI extends our capacity to meet members; it is never the meeting itself.

  2. 02

    Members own their stories. We hold the minimum, in infrastructure we control, and member data never trains anyone's model.

  3. 03

    A human answers for everything. Nothing publishes on autopilot, and no machine decides anything that matters about a person.

  4. 04

    We say what's synthetic. Avatars are presented as avatars; AI contributions are disclosed; no fake voices, ever.

  5. 05

    We steer the defaults. Left alone, these tools erase us — correcting that drift is part of the work, not an extra.

  6. 06

    Consent is the line between care and surveillance. Members tell us what they need; we don't mine them for it.

  7. 07

    Different, not worse. None of the engagement machinery of the platforms we exist as an alternative to.

Five red lines — not aspirations

  • Member data never trains anyone's model. Not sold, not shared, not contributed — and every processor we use is configured to the same standard.
  • No surveillance dressed as care. No profiling, no engagement scoring, no inferring what a member hasn't chosen to tell us.
  • No machine decisions about people. Joining, moderation, money, access — a human makes the actual decision and is accountable for it.
  • No simulated intimacy. AI does not provide counselling, crisis response or pastoral care in human disguise. Where someone needs a person, they get a person.
  • No engagement machinery. No attention-maximising, no manufactured urgency, no dark patterns. Not the same machine with better politics.

Why we use AI at all

BLKOUT is a small co-operative with a large purpose: digital infrastructure for Black queer liberation. AI lets a handful of people run a platform, a newsroom, an events calendar and a membership offer that would otherwise need a staff team we don't have and funders won't pay for. We use it the way a community kitchen uses industrial equipment — to multiply what care can reach, never to replace the cook.

We are not neutral about these tools. They are built by corporations whose values are not ours, trained on data extracted without consent, and tuned to defaults that erase people like us. We use them anyway, eyes open, because the alternative — Black queer infrastructure that can't afford to exist — serves no one. This policy is how we stay honest about that bargain.

What AI is for at BLKOUT

Extending capacity, not simulating care. AI may draft, research, summarise, illustrate, schedule, transcribe, translate and build. It may help us notice a member's signal sooner and respond better. It may never be the response where the promise was a person. Our membership covenant — to meet people reliably — is kept by humans. AI helps us keep it; it doesn't keep it for us.

Production with human editorship. Every AI-assisted output that reaches the community — an image, a script, a newsletter, a video — passes through human editorial judgement before it ships, and a named person answers for it. Nothing publishes on autopilot.

Steering the defaults. Left alone, generative tools produce a world that is whiter, straighter, sadder and more generic than ours. When we use them we actively correct: towards creation rather than loss, the full range of Black queer life and age, specificity over stock imagery, "we learn" over "we fail." This is a standing production obligation, not a preference.

Honest representation. AIvor is an avatar — stylised, framed as broadcast, named in homage to Ivor Cummings — and is always presented as what he is. We do not pass synthetic people off as real ones. No fake members, no fabricated testimony, no AI-generated "community voices," no deepfakes of real people. Where AI contributed materially to a published work, we say so plainly.

Data sovereignty

Members own their stories. We hold the minimum data needed to keep our promises, in infrastructure we control, and we are building towards members seeing, editing and deleting everything we hold about them. Any AI processing of member data happens under these same rules — minimum necessary, named purpose, never for training, never for sale.

Governance and accountability

The board owns this policy and reviews it annually, or sooner if practice or tools change materially. The technical lead maintains the public register below, updated as the stack changes. Any member can challenge any use of AI at BLKOUT and get an answer from a human — through any channel we run, with the board as the backstop. And when we get it wrong, we say so. A lesson, published, beats a failure concealed. That is how the whole platform works; AI gets no exemption.

The thinking
behind the building

A growing series on what liberation technology asks of us. Three to start.

01 · The frame

Ancestral intelligence

The platforms have an intelligence. It ranks, it mines, it erases. It decides which of our stories surface and which sink, guided by nothing more principled than what keeps a thumb moving. We are told this intelligence is artificial. It is better described as extractive — and Black queer memory is among the things it extracts.

We propose a different grounding. Ancestral intelligence: the knowledge, survival craft and cultural forms carried by Black queer ancestors and elders — and this community's right to hold, interpret and transmit them itself. Not nostalgia. Infrastructure. Ivor Cummings, the man our avatar honours, made a practice of opening doors for newly arrived Black Britons who had nobody else to call. That is a technology of care. It predates the smartphone by seventy years and outperforms most of what runs on one.

Why does the distinction matter? Because a dispersed minority faces a particular double bind. It is easier to be heard through non-minority interlocutors — mainstream platforms, mainstream gatekeepers — but what arrives on the other side is diluted. So we choose between dismissing the mainstreamed version of ourselves as inauthentic, or doing the unpaid work of re-applying the specificity the mainstreaming stripped away. Either way, the platform takes its cut. Meanwhile the people holding the most living memory are ageing, and their archives sit in shoeboxes, on dying hard drives, on accounts one terms-of-service change from deletion.

BLKOUT began in the lineage of Black queer publishing — Barbara Smith's Kitchen Table: Women of Color Press was making liberation infrastructure with a typesetter long before anyone said "platform." Reparative media practice is our continuation of that lineage: gather the scattered memory, return the agency of recording and interpreting to the community it documents, and hold it in infrastructure the community actually owns. A commons, cooperatively governed, with an asset lock — so it cannot quietly become somebody's exit.

This is decolonising work, and we are clear-eyed that decolonial language is itself a target for recuperation — every extractive product now arrives wearing the vocabulary of empowerment. The test is not the language. The test is who holds, who interprets, who transmits. Ancestral intelligence answers: we do.

02 · The practice

Steer the defaults

Ask a generative model for Black queer men at a picnic and watch what comes back. Younger than us. Sadder than us. Somewhere that is recognisably nowhere, in light borrowed from a pharmaceutical advert. Run it again and the picnic greys towards a memorial. The tools do not hate us. They have simply averaged a world that under-imagined us, and an average is a politics wearing a lab coat.

Left alone, generative tools produce a world that is whiter, straighter, sadder and more generic than ours. That sentence sits in our board-adopted AI policy — principle five — and as far as we can tell, no other organisation's AI policy names it. Most policies worry about what these tools might say. Ours also worries about what they erase by default, because erasure is the failure mode we know best.

So steering is a standing production obligation at BLKOUT. Not a preference. Not a nice-to-have when deadlines allow. When we generate images, Black figures are anchored to reference photographs so the tool cannot drift us towards ambiguity. We correct towards creation rather than loss — our community is building things, and the imagery should say so. We insist on the full range of Black queer life and age; we were not all born in 1998, and the elders who made this community possible deserve better than to be uninventable. We choose "we learn" over "we fail," specificity over stock. And every image that ships carries our mark and passes a human eye first.

Is this a lot of work? Yes. That is rather the point. The platforms sold us the fantasy of frictionless production — type a phrase, receive a world. But the friction we removed was where the judgement lived. When the default output erases you, accepting the default is not neutrality. It is agreement on a discount.

There is a wider argument here, and it belongs to everyone building with these tools, not just us. Defaults are political. Somebody chose the training data; somebody tuned what "professional" looks like; somebody decided which bodies read as "beautiful" without a modifier. Refusing the default is available to any organisation willing to name the drift and budget the correction. We publish our approach so the refusal spreads. The tools will keep offering us a world without us in it. We decline — in the settings, in the prompts, in the edit, every single time.

03 · The line

Consent is the line between care and surveillance

The nod. Two Black men pass on a street that holds no other Black faces, and one tilts his head a careful few degrees. Nothing is said. Everything is said: I see you, you're not alone here. What makes the nod care rather than intrusion is that it asks nothing and takes nothing. It offers, and it lets the offer be declined.

The platforms built the opposite. They watch everything — what you linger on, what you almost typed, who you looked at twice — infer what you never chose to share, and sell the inference to whoever bids. They call this personalisation, and its warmth is the warmth of a heat lamp: engineered, and pointed at your wallet. Surveillance dressed as care is our policy's second red line, and it earns that placing because our community knows the costume well. We have been profiled by states, by health systems, by apps that promised discretion and delivered a data breach.

So where is the line, for an organisation that genuinely wants to notice people? Consent. Care asks; surveillance assumes. Members tell us what they need — we don't mine them for it.

Here is what that looks like in our actual plumbing. When a man makes his first gesture towards BLKOUT — claims a code, saves a picnic spot, writes in the box at the foot of this page — that gesture is recorded for exactly one purpose: so a human being answers him, and answers within days, remembering what he actually did. That is the whole system. We built instrumentation not to score people but so that no one who reaches towards this community gets silence back. The technology's job is to make sure care doesn't drop things. The care itself stays human.

Now the refusals, because the refusals are the design. No profiling. No engagement scores. No inferring a man's health, desires or troubles from his click patterns. No algorithm deciding he looks lonely and serving him an advert shaped like a friend. If we want to know something, we ask, plainly, and he can decline — the way you can decline a nod by simply walking on, no penalty, no note kept.

Even this page practises the argument. The respond box below tells you exactly what we keep and the single use it's put to. No analytics script counted your scroll depth to this paragraph. Different, not worse — and on this measure, honestly? Better.
